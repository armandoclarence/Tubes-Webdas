Forbidden City History

The grand chronicle of the Forbidden City began in 1406, when Emperor Yongle of the Ming Dynasty decreed the relocation of China's capital northward to Beijing. To project absolute cosmic authority, he commissioned a monumental palace complex whose construction spanned 14 years and required the labor of over one million workers and artisans. The compound was named Zijincheng (The Purple Forbidden City) because its grounds were sacred; commoners were strictly barred from approaching or entering without authorization from the Emperor.

For nearly five centuries, this majestic palace functioned as the absolute political heart of China. It witnessed the historical rise and eventual fall of two great ruling lines: the Ming Dynasty (of ethnic Han heritage) and later the Qing Dynasty (of ethnic Manchu heritage). In total, 24 emperors lived, worked, and ruled from behind these towering crimson walls, shaping the destinies of millions through imperial edicts signed within the courts.

On October 10, 1925, the gates that had remained tightly sealed for centuries were opened to the public for the first time. The complex transitioned into a public space, officially designated as The Palace Museum. Today, what was once a realm shrouded in absolute secrecy and reverence has transformed into one of the world's most visited digital heritage museums and a protected UNESCO World Heritage Site, welcoming global visitors to marvel at China's rich past.
