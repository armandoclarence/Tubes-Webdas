Forbidden City History

The grand chronicle of the Forbidden City (Zijincheng) began in 1406 under the decree of Emperor Yongle of the Ming Dynasty. Conceived as an earthly manifestation of the celestial realms, this monumental complex served as China's absolute political and spiritual gravity center for over five centuries. An intriguing architectural anomaly revealed by historians is that the palace’s north-south symmetrical axis is intentionally tilted by slightly more than 2 degrees. This deliberate misalignment was engineered during the preceding Yuan Dynasty to align perfectly with Shangdu, their secondary imperial capital.

1406 – 1420

The Grand Construction Phase

Massive construction era ordered by Emperor Yongle of the Ming Dynasty after deciding to relocate the primary imperial capital to Beijing. The process required processing timber from distant southwestern forests and stone slabs hauled over ice tracks.

1420 – 1644

The Height of Ming Sovereignty

The complex functioned as the highly fortified, absolute center of governance for the Han-led Ming Dynasty. During this era, strictly structured administrative and civil service selection processes dictated palace movements.

1644

The Dynastic Fire & Transition

Rebel forces led by Li Zicheng captured and burned core sectors of the palace structures. This volatile moment broke Ming authority and allowed the Manchu-led Qing Dynasty to capture the throne.

1644 – 1912

The Qing Administrative Consolidation

The seat of sovereign administrative power for the Qing Dynasty. Architecture choices combined traditional Manchu lifestyles with Han cultural foundations to govern a changing empire.

1912 – 1924

The Post-Revolution Twilight

Following the Xinhai Revolution, the Last Emperor Puyi abdicated but was permitted to reside within the private Northern Inner Court under special state favor agreements.

1925 – Present

Democratization as The Palace Museum

On October 10, 1925, the tightly sealed imperial gates opened wide to the general public as The Palace Museum. The complex transitioned into a public space, democratizing a legacy once completely inaccessible.
